Hoya lacunosa ‘Snow Caps’ — the silver-splash wax vine

A small-leafed wax vine wearing a blizzard: every lance-shaped leaf is speckled, splashed, and sometimes drowned in silver, so the whole basket shimmers from deep green to near-white. The splash is not paint and not damage — it deepens in bright light, and a mature plant answers good light with fuzzy white flowers that smell of cinnamon.

🔍 Anatomy — Telling It from Its Heart-Leafed Cousin

THE FLOWERS
fuzzy white umbels

Two small-leafed hoyas are forever confused in the trade, and the honest divider is the leaf base. H. krohniana — split out of lacunosa in 2009 — carries consistently heart-shaped leaves, small, thick, and stiff, every base notched like a valentine. Your plant’s leaves are lance-shaped, about three times long as wide, tapering to a long point, with wedge-shaped bases and thin, flexible blades — the lacunosa pattern, confirmed leaf by leaf across your photo. The splash on yours runs to the extreme end of the scale, whole leaves silvered to near-white: that is exactly the selection sold as ‘Snow Caps’. A word of honesty: the ‘Snow Caps’ name is hung on heart-leafed plants too — the label alone proves nothing. This identification rests on the leaves, not the tag.

🌿 The names: lacunosa means “full of little hollows” — the leaf surface dimples between its veins. ‘Snow Caps’ is for the snow-dusted leaves, and growers also call this species the cinnamon hoya, for the scent of its flowers. The heart-leafed cousin spent a century filed as “heart-leafed lacunosa” before Kloppenburg & Siar gave it its own name, H. krohniana, in 2009.

🎛️ Conditions — Measured

In the wild this is an epiphyte — it lives perched on trees in the lowland forests of India, Thailand, and Malaysia, roots in bark litter, leaves in dappled sun. Grow the idea, not the jungle: bright light is the whole ballgame — it keeps the growth compact, the splash silver, and the flower buds coming. A specialist grower runs this species at about 400 foot-candles under lights, 14 hours a day; a bright east or west windowsill is the home version. Gentle morning sun is fine; hard midday sun through glass can scorch.

📋 The Care Card

LightBright indirect light all day — an east or west window, or a south window with the basket set back or sheer-filtered. Aim for roughly 10,000–20,000 lux at the leaves. More light means more silver; too little and new growth comes in plain green and leggy.
WaterWater thoroughly, drain fully, then wait until the mix is 60–80% dry before watering again — typically every 1–2 weeks in growth, less in winter. The thin roots rot fast in a wet, dense mix; when in doubt, wait a few days.
SoilAn airy epiphyte mix: orchid bark, perlite or pumice, and coco coir — about half chunky mineral and bark, half organic. Small pot, drainage holes, no standing water in the saucer.
FeedingA light, balanced feed at low dose through spring and summer — every watering at quarter strength, or monthly at half strength — with an occasional plain-water flush. Little to nothing in winter.
TemperatureHappy at 60–85°F. Keep it above 50°F at all times; cold plus wet is the fatal pairing. Outdoors only in Zones 10–11.
FlowersUmbels of small, fuzzy, cream-white stars with yellow centers, strongly cinnamon-sweet, strongest at night, in spring and summer on a mature plant. The flowers grow on short stalks called peduncles that stay and rebloom for years — never cut the peduncles off, even when they look bare.
RepottingRarely, and reluctantly — hoyas bloom best a little snug. Go one size up in spring only when roots crowd the pot or the mix has collapsed and drains slowly.

✂️ Propagation — Cuttings Root Willingly

  1. Cut a healthy stem with at least one or two nodes — the bumps where leaves join. Roots and new growth come from the nodes, so a leaf alone will never make a vine.
  2. Let the cut end callus for a few hours. The milky sap will weep; a rinse and a pause keep it out of the mix.
  3. Root it your way: in water, in damp sphagnum moss with perlite, or laid into the same airy mix as the mother plant, node in contact with the medium.
  4. Keep it warm and humid — a loose bag or prop box over the pot — until roots run an inch or two, usually 3–4 weeks. Then pot on and grow it like the parent.
  5. Share or refill: rooted cuttings tucked back around the mother’s rim are the fastest route to a fuller basket.

🩺 Troubleshooting

Plain green, leggy new growthToo little light — the splash is light-made. Move it brighter, gradually.
Buds form, then drop unopenedDry air, drafts, or a move mid-bud. Raise humidity toward 50–70% and leave the plant be while it buds.
Yellow, mushy leaves; sour mixRoot rot from overwatering — unpot, cut away black roots, repot into fresh airy mix, water lightly.
White cottony tufts in leaf jointsMealybugs — the hoya pest. Dab off with rubbing alcohol on a swab and recheck weekly; also watch for their root-dwelling cousins at repot time.
Fine webbing, stippled leavesSpider mites, usually in dry air — shower the leaves, raise humidity, and treat if they persist. Aphids favor soft new tips; rinse them off.
Healthy plant, never flowersAlmost always light, or patience — and check that no one trimmed the bare peduncles, which are next season’s flower stalks.
🐈

Safe around animals. The ASPCA lists the wax-plant hoyas — Hoya carnosa, H. kerrii, H. pubicalyx — as non-toxic to cats, dogs, and horses, and no hoya appears on its toxic list; H. lacunosa simply has no entry of its own. Any plant eaten in quantity can still upset a stomach, so the hanging basket is the right home for it — beautiful, and out of nibbling range. ASPCA Poison Control: (888) 426-4435.

❄️ Reading Your Photo

Your basket hangs against a pale clay wall over a black surface — a gallery setup the plant deserves, and the dark ground sets off the silver exactly the way a jeweler’s cloth sets off a stone. Give it the brightest spot that setup allows, a drink only when the mix has mostly dried, and leave every bare little flower stalk in place: next spring’s cinnamon is already on them.
