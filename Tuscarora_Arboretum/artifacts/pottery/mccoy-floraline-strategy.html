McCoy's Floraline Strategy

How an Ohio pottery, squeezed by imported glass and cheap imports, bet its survival on flower shops.

1910Nelson McCoy founded, Roseville, Ohio
1960Floraline launches, June 9
1967Family sells the pottery
1990The doors finally close

The Squeeze

By the late 1950s the American pottery industry was being ground down from two directions. Imported ware — first European, then Japanese — undercut Ohio prices on every store shelf, and glass had become the cheap, clear, modern default container: a florist could buy imported glass vases for less than a fired, glazed, shipped ceramic one. McCoy's own history tells the arc — the collectors' references describe the 1960s plainly as a spiral of decline as cheaper overseas imports made profit harder every year. The company's famous lines, the cookie jars America loved, were complicated, loss-prone pieces fighting a price war they could not win.

Nelson McCoy's answer was not a better cookie jar. It was a different customer.

The Gambit — June 9, 1960

"Nelson McCoy was looking for an economical line of pottery that could be sold directly to individual florist shops. In fact the name 'Floraline' was named for this purpose." — McCoy Pottery's Floraline line history

1 · Sell to the trade, not the shelf

Floraline was sold directly to florist shops — a trade-only line. No department-store shelf war against imports; the buyer was a professional who ordered containers by the case, every week, forever. Formally it even carried its own name: Roseville Floraline.

2 · Design that serves the flowers

The design doctrine was restraint: "simple form with classic lines, with solid eye pleasing colors that could accentuate the floral designs that would be placed in them rather than detract from them." The pot was never the product — the arrangement was. Florists noticed.

3 · Cheap to make, hard to ruin

The line was "inexpensive and simple to produce — any McCoy employee knew the line and could make it with a minimal amount of loss, unlike cookie jars and more complicated designs." Simple numbered molds, matte solid glazes, low breakage: ceramic that could finally meet glass on price.

4 · A system, not a catalog of pieces

Every piece carried a mold number and its own Floraline mark on the base — a florist could reorder "a case of 424s" the way you'd reorder any supply. Pedestals, compotes, bud vases and planters in a shared palette of whites, greens, yellows and earth tones covered weddings, funerals, hospitals and holidays from one system.

How It Ended

1910 — Nelson McCoy Sr., with his father J.W. and five stockholders, founds the Nelson McCoy Sanitary Stoneware Co. in Roseville, Ohio, on local clay.
1933 — Utility ware fades; the company renames itself the Nelson McCoy Pottery Co. and goes decorative — the cookie-jar era follows.
1960 — Floraline launches June 9: trade-only, florist-direct, built to beat imported glass on price and service.
1967 — After 57 family years, the pottery is sold to the owners of the Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.; Nelson McCoy Jr. stays on as president.
1974 — Lancaster Colony Corporation buys the pottery. The Collectors Society records that Floraline's marketing strategy changed severely soon after, and the line ended shortly thereafter.
1985 — Sold again, to Designer Accents of New Jersey; production of several potteries is consolidated at Roseville.
1990 — Designer Accents closes the doors for good. The office building burns the following fall.
The honest verdict: Floraline did not save American pottery — nothing could have. What it did was give one Ohio factory a defensible business for its hardest decades: a professional customer imports couldn't easily take, ordering a simple product at a fair price, by number, week after week. The pedestal planter on a Martinsburg windowsill is that strategy, still holding a plant sixty years on.
